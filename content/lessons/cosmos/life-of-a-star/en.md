## Try it

1. Set «star's mass» to 1 (the Sun) and drag «age» past 1.1. Watch the star swell into a red giant, then shrink to a small white dwarf.
2. Push «star's mass» above 20 and drag «age» to the far right. The star flashes as a supernova and leaves a black hole behind.
3. Drop «star's mass» below 0.5 and drag «age» all the way across. A red dwarf barely changes — it would take trillions of years to get this far.

## Real-life examples

1. **Proxima Centauri.** The nearest star to the Sun has a mass of about 0.12 M☉. Burning this slowly, it will shine for roughly two trillion years — far longer than the 13.8-billion-year-old universe has existed.
2. **The Sun.** At 1 M☉ and about 4.6 billion years old, the Sun is roughly 46% of the way through its 10-billion-year main-sequence life.
3. **Betelgeuse.** This red supergiant in Orion has a mass of about 18 M☉. It already left the main sequence and is puffed up hundreds of times wider than the Sun — a strong candidate to explode as a supernova, maybe within the next 100,000 years.

## Intuition

A star is a battle between gravity, which is always trying to crush it, and the pressure from nuclear fusion in its core, which pushes back. A heavier star has to fuse hydrogen much faster to hold up its own weight, so it burns fiercely and finishes quickly. A light star can take it easy, sipping fuel for ages. That single fact — mass sets the pace — decides everything else about a star's life.

Drag «star's mass» up and the model star turns hotter and bluer, because a heavier star's core runs hotter to support the extra weight above it. Drag «age» forward and you fast-forward through that star's whole life: a calm middle stretch called the main sequence, then a short, dramatic ending that depends entirely on how much mass the star started with.

## Formal

Along the main sequence, a star's luminosity climbs steeply with its mass, roughly $L = M^{3.5}$ in solar units — double the mass and the star puts out about eleven times the light. Since a star's fuel supply is roughly proportional to its mass while it burns that fuel at a rate set by $L$, its lifetime is $t \approx M/L$.

Combining those two relations gives $t = 10\,M^{-2.5}$, with $t$ in billions of years. The Sun, at $M=1$, gets almost exactly the standard 10-billion-year figure quoted for a Sun-like star. A star twice as heavy lives roughly $2^{-2.5} \approx 18\%$ as long — heavier stars are dramatically shorter-lived, not longer.

## Advanced

The mass thresholds that split a star's fate are approximate but well established: below about 0.5 M☉, a red dwarf stays on the main sequence for longer than the current age of the universe. Between about 0.5 and 8 M☉, a star swells into a red giant, sheds its outer layers, and leaves behind an Earth-sized white dwarf. Above about 8 M☉, the core keeps fusing all the way to iron, which cannot release more energy — the core collapses in under a second, and the star explodes as a core-collapse supernova, leaving a neutron star (below roughly 20 M☉) or a black hole (above it).

## Derivation

1. Along the main sequence, a star's luminosity rises steeply with its mass — a well-tested approximation astronomers call the mass–luminosity relation.
2. A star's lifetime is roughly how much fuel it has (proportional to its mass) divided by how fast it burns that fuel (its luminosity).
3. Combining those two relations shows the lifetime falls off sharply with mass: a star twice as heavy as the Sun lives only a fifth as long.

## Real world

### Supernova 1987A
In February 1987, astronomers watched a blue supergiant about 20 times the Sun's mass explode in a nearby galaxy — the nearest supernova seen in centuries, and the first time neutrinos from a collapsing core were detected on Earth.

### Dating star clusters
Astronomers estimate a star cluster's age by finding which stars have already left the main sequence: the heaviest surviving main-sequence star sets a maximum age, since anything heavier would already be gone.

### Where heavy elements come from
Supernovae forge and scatter elements heavier than iron — including much of the gold, silver, and uranium on Earth — so a massive star's violent death seeds the next generation of planets.

### White dwarfs as cosmic clocks
Because white dwarfs cool at a known, steady rate, measuring how cool one has become tells astronomers how long ago its parent star died.
