## Try it

1. Drag «matter swallowed per year» up. The brighter the feeding, the higher the curve climbs.
2. Raise «efficiency η» instead. Even the same feeding rate now shines far brighter.
3. Push both sliders high together and read the luminosity — some real quasars reach tens of trillions of Suns.

## Real-life examples

1. **A quasar like 3C 273.** Swallowing about 2 Suns' worth of matter a year at 10% efficiency, it still shines brighter than an entire galaxy.
2. **A quiet, weakly fed black hole.** At only 0.3 Suns per year and low efficiency, the glow barely rises above the baseline.
3. **An extremely luminous early-universe quasar.** Feeding on 8 Suns a year at a high 30% efficiency, this monster outshines its host galaxy many times over.

## Intuition

3C 273, discovered in 1963, sits about 2.4 billion light-years away yet still looks like a faint dot through a small telescope — because it outshines its entire host galaxy, blazing at roughly 4 trillion times the Sun's brightness from a region smaller than our solar system.

Drag «matter swallowed per year» up on the picture and the curve climbs steeply — gas and dust spiralling into a black hole heats up and glows fiercely before it ever crosses the point of no return. Raise «efficiency η» as well, and the same infalling matter now yields even more light, since a bigger share of its mass-energy manages to escape as radiation.

## Formal

Einstein's mass-energy relation, $E = mc^2$, says that mass itself is a store of energy. A quasar taps that store as matter falls into a supermassive black hole, releasing luminosity $L = \eta \dot M c^2$, where $\dot M$ is the mass falling in each second and $\eta$ is the fraction that escapes as light rather than crossing the horizon.

Converting to convenient units — $\dot M$ in Suns per year and $L$ in trillions of Suns — this relation becomes the simple rule plotted here, $L \approx 14.8\,\eta\,\dot M$.

## Advanced

Typical efficiencies of 5–30%, set by how close a black hole's spin lets infalling matter orbit before plunging in, already dwarf the roughly 0.7% efficiency of nuclear fusion inside stars, which is why accretion onto a black hole is the most efficient known way of turning mass into light.

## Derivation

1. Mass itself is a form of energy, so matter falling toward a black hole carries a store of energy that can be released.
2. Only a fraction of that infalling mass-energy actually escapes as light rather than disappearing across the black hole's horizon.
3. Converting to solar masses per year and trillions of solar luminosities turns that relation into the simple rule used here.

## Real world

### The first quasar, 3C 273
Identified in 1963 by Maarten Schmidt, its puzzlingly large redshift revealed it was billions of light-years away, far too bright for its distance under ordinary explanations.

### Quasars as cosmic lighthouses
Because they are so bright, quasars let astronomers study gas in galaxies billions of light-years away, using their light as a backlight.

### Sagittarius A*, our own quiet giant
The supermassive black hole at our galaxy's center feeds so slowly today that it is millions of times fainter than a typical quasar.

### Shaping galaxy growth
A quasar's radiation and outflowing wind can blow away the gas a galaxy needs to form new stars, linking a black hole's growth to its galaxy's fate.
