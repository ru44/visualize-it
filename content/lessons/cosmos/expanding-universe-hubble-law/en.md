## Real-life examples

1. **Matter-only toy universe, today.** With Ωm at 1 and ΩΛ at 0, gravity from ordinary matter is the only thing acting, and it slows the expansion down over time.
2. **An empty, coasting universe.** With both Ωm and ΩΛ at 0, there is nothing to speed up or slow down the expansion — galaxies just drift apart at a constant rate.
3. **A universe like ours.** About 30% matter and 70% dark energy: gravity slowed the expansion at first, then dark energy took over and it started speeding up again.

## Intuition

Andromeda, our nearest big neighbouring galaxy, is only 0.78 Mpc away and is actually drifting toward us. But almost every other galaxy is moving away, and a galaxy 100 Mpc away recedes at roughly 7000 km/s — about ten times faster than one only 10 Mpc away.

Drag «point in cosmic time» forward and watch the galaxy dots spread apart on the picture. The farther two dots start out, the faster the gap between them grows, exactly like Andromeda's neighbours: distance and speed climb together.

## Formal

The Hubble parameter measures how fast the universe's scale factor is growing: $H(t) = \dot a/a$. At any instant, the proper distance to a galaxy is $d = a\,d_0$, where $d_0$ is a fixed reference distance and $a$ is the scale factor.

Differentiating that distance with respect to time and evaluating today, when $a=1$, gives Hubble's law: $v = H_0 d$. A galaxy twice as far away recedes twice as fast, no matter which galaxy you start from.

## Advanced

Edwin Hubble measured this relation in 1929 using just a few dozen galaxies and a value of $H_0$ several times too large; today's best estimates cluster around 67–73 km/s/Mpc, with the small remaining disagreement between methods known as the Hubble tension. The recession is not galaxies flying through space but space itself stretching between them, which is why speeds faster than light are allowed for the most distant galaxies without breaking relativity.

## Derivation

1. The Hubble parameter is defined as the fractional rate at which the universe's scale factor is growing at any moment.
2. A galaxy's proper distance at any time is today's reference distance scaled up by that same growing factor.
3. Differentiating distance with time, and evaluating today, gives recession speed proportional to distance — Hubble's law.

## Real world

### Measuring redshift
Astronomers infer a galaxy's recession speed from how much its light has stretched toward the red end of the spectrum, a direct fingerprint of the expansion.

### Building the cosmic distance ladder
Hubble's law, calibrated against nearer distance measurements, lets astronomers estimate distances to galaxies far too far away to measure any other way.

### Naming the Hubble Space Telescope
The telescope launched in 1990 was named after Edwin Hubble for this discovery, and has since refined the constant that bears his name.
