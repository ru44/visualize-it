## Real-life examples

1. **A small dwarf galaxy.** Only a light halo, around 2, is enough to keep its slow gas clouds from drifting apart.
2. **A spiral galaxy like the Milky Way.** A halo near 5, checked out to 20 kpc, matches the flat rotation astronomers actually see.
3. **A massive elliptical galaxy.** Even 25 kpc from the center, a halo near 8 keeps the outer stars moving far faster than visible matter alone allows.

## Intuition

The Sun circles the center of the Milky Way at about 220 km/s, some 8 kpc out. Neptune, far out in our own solar system, crawls at only 5 km/s, because the Sun's pull is so much weaker that far away. Stars in other galaxies should slow down the same way the farther out they sit — but when astronomers measured them, most did not.

Drag «distance from center r» outward and watch the black measured points: they stay at roughly the same height instead of dropping. Raise «dark matter halo» from 0 and the orange curve rises to meet them. The only way to keep those far-out stars moving that fast is extra mass, spread through and beyond the visible galaxy, that no telescope has ever photographed.

## Formal

A star circling at distance $r$ needs gravity to supply its inward pull, which fixes its orbital speed at $v = \sqrt{GM(r)/r}$. Here $M(r)$ is all the mass sitting inside radius $r$, and $G$ is Newton's gravitational constant.

If only the matter astronomers can photograph existed, $M(r)$ would stop growing past the edge of the visible disk, and $v$ would fall off with distance. Real rotation curves stay flat instead, so $M(r)$ must keep climbing in step with $r$ itself — mass with no light of its own, filling a halo around the galaxy.

## Advanced

Fitting curves like this one across thousands of galaxies, pioneered by Vera Rubin in the 1970s, is one of the strongest pieces of evidence for dark matter. Combined with gravitational lensing and the cosmic microwave background, it points to a form of matter that interacts through gravity but not light, and that outweighs ordinary matter roughly five to one.

## Derivation

1. Setting gravity equal to the pull needed for a circular orbit ties a star's speed to the mass enclosed within its orbit.
2. Solving that balance for the orbital speed gives the rotation-curve formula.
3. If the measured speed stays roughly constant as r grows, the enclosed mass must grow in proportion to r itself, far past where the visible galaxy ends.

## Real world

### Vera Rubin's galaxies
In the 1970s Vera Rubin and Kent Ford measured dozens of spiral galaxies and found their outer stars orbiting far too fast for the visible matter — one of the first clear signs of dark matter.

### Weighing a galaxy without seeing its mass
Astronomers use curves like this one to estimate how much dark matter surrounds a galaxy, long before any telescope could hope to see the halo directly.

### Dwarf galaxies as extreme test cases
Small, faint dwarf galaxies often show the strongest evidence of all, since their visible matter is far too little to explain how fast their gas moves.
