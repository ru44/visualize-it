## Intuition

Tap across period 3, from sodium to chlorine shown here, and picture the nucleus gaining one more proton at every step while the inner electron shells stay the same size. Each valence electron feels a steadily stronger net pull, so the atom shrinks, holds its electrons more tightly, and becomes harder to strip an electron from — its ionisation energy climbs, and so does its pull on shared electrons in a bond, its electronegativity.

Now compare down a group instead, say from fluorine to chlorine to bromine. Each step adds a whole new shell further from the nucleus, and that extra distance outweighs the modest rise in nuclear charge: the atom gets bigger, its outer electrons are held more loosely, and both ionisation energy and electronegativity fall. Size grows down, tightens across — the two trends run at right angles to each other on the table.

## Formal

Atomic radius shrinks left-to-right across a period and grows top-to-bottom down a group. First ionisation energy — the energy to remove the outermost electron — does the reverse: it rises across a period (tighter grip, harder to remove) and falls down a group (looser grip, easier to remove), with the noble gases at the peak of each period. Helium has the highest first ionisation energy of any element, a direct consequence of its very small, tightly-bound outer shell.

Electronegativity, the tendency of an atom to pull shared electrons toward itself in a bond, follows ionisation energy closely for the same reason: fluorine has the highest electronegativity of any element, 3.98 on the Pauling scale, while the large alkali metals at the bottom-left of the table, caesium among them, sit among the lowest. The two trends together make the top-right corner of the table (excluding the noble gases) the most electron-hungry region, and the bottom-left the most electron-giving.

## Advanced

These trends are not perfectly smooth: small irregularities appear where a half-filled or fully-filled subshell gives extra stability, so a handful of elements have a slightly higher or lower ionisation energy than their neighbour would suggest. The overall direction across a period and down a group is reliable and is what most chemistry is built on, but exact numerical predictions need the real measured values rather than the simple $Z_{eff} = Z - S$ picture alone.

## Derivation

1. Effective nuclear charge is the actual charge Z reduced by how much the inner electrons shield it.
2. Moving across a period adds protons to Z while the shielding from inner shells barely changes, so $Z_{eff}$ rises.
3. Moving down a group adds a whole shell, and the extra distance from the nucleus outweighs the modest rise in $Z_{eff}$, so the atom gets bigger.

## Real world

### Why fluorine is such a fierce oxidiser
Fluorine's extreme electronegativity means it pulls electrons out of almost any other atom, making it one of the most reactive elements known.

### Caesium in atomic clocks
Caesium's very low ionisation energy — easy to strip an electron from — is part of why it is easy to probe and manipulate, and its transition frequency defines the second.

### Predicting bond polarity
Knowing where two elements sit on the electronegativity trend tells a chemist, before running any experiment, which atom in a bond will carry the partial negative charge.

### Doping semiconductors
Chip manufacturers choose dopant atoms partly by atomic radius, so the added atom fits into the host crystal's lattice without excessive strain.
