## Try it

1. Tap through the periodic table to change «atomic number Z». Watch the valence-electron count and the element's family change.
2. Land on a metal from the left side, then a nonmetal from the right. Notice how far apart their electronegativities sit.
3. Land on two nonmetals close together on the table and compare — their electronegativities are close, favouring a shared, covalent bond.

## Real-life examples

1. **Table salt, sodium chloride.** Sodium (Z = 11) has one lonely valence electron it gives up readily — paired with a strongly electronegative nonmetal it forms a solid ionic crystal.
2. **Fluoride in toothpaste and drinking water.** Fluorine (Z = 9) is the most electronegative element there is, pulling electrons from metals to form strong ionic compounds like calcium fluoride.
3. **Carbon in every organic molecule.** Carbon (Z = 6) shares electrons about evenly with hydrogen and other nonmetals, building the covalent backbones of sugars, fats and DNA.

## Intuition

Tap through the table to fluorine, shown here: it has seven valence electrons and is one short of a stable, full outer shell of eight. An atom like sodium, one shell bigger, has the opposite problem — a single lonely valence electron it would rather lose than keep. Put the two together and sodium simply hands its electron to fluorine: sodium becomes a positive ion, fluorine a negative one, and the electrical attraction between them is an ionic bond.

Now imagine two atoms that both want to keep their electrons about equally, such as two chlorine atoms, or carbon and hydrogen. Neither can win a tug-of-war for a whole electron, so instead they share a pair, each contributing one electron and both counting it toward their own full shell. That shared pair, held in the space between the two nuclei, is a covalent bond — the electrons never fully belong to one atom or the other.

## Formal

Both bond types are driven by the octet rule: atoms are most stable with eight electrons (two for hydrogen and helium) in their outermost shell, matching the nearest noble gas. Ionic bonding reaches that state by complete electron transfer, forming charged ions held together by electrostatic attraction; covalent bonding reaches it by sharing electron pairs, so no full transfer occurs.

Which one happens is predicted by the electronegativity difference between the two atoms, $\Delta\chi = |\chi_1 - \chi_2|$. A large difference — a metal from the left of the table paired with a nonmetal from the right, as in sodium and fluorine — pulls the shared electron almost entirely onto one atom, which is effectively a transfer: an ionic bond. A small or zero difference, as between two nonmetals or two identical atoms, leaves the electron pair genuinely shared: a covalent bond.

## Advanced

Real bonds are rarely purely one or the other; even many "ionic" bonds have some shared electron density, and many "covalent" bonds are polar, with the pair sitting closer to the more electronegative atom and giving that end a partial negative charge. Electronegativity difference is best read as a sliding scale from nonpolar covalent through polar covalent to ionic, not three separate boxes.

## Derivation

1. An atom's valence-electron count, and so how far it is from a full octet, is set by its position in the table.
2. The electronegativity difference between two bonding atoms measures how unevenly they would pull on a shared electron pair.
3. A large difference favours outright transfer (ionic); a small one favours sharing (covalent).

## Real world

### Why table salt dissolves in water
Sodium chloride is held together by strong ionic attraction between Na⁺ and Cl⁻, but water's polar molecules surround and separate those ions easily, which is why salt dissolves readily.

### Why methane is a gas at room temperature
Methane's covalent C–H bonds create small, electrically near-neutral molecules with only weak attractions between them, so little energy is needed to separate them into a gas.

### Metals and alloys
Metallic bonding, a third case where valence electrons are shared across many atoms at once rather than between just two, explains why metals conduct electricity and can be bent without shattering.

### Body salts and nerve signals
Ionic compounds like sodium and potassium chloride dissolve into free ions in the body, and the flow of those ions across cell membranes is how nerve cells send signals.
