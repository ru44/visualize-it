## Intuition

Tap across a row (a period) and watch the element change while its position stays on the same horizontal band. Each period corresponds to one more occupied electron shell than the one above it: period 1 has only one shell, period 2 has two, and so on down to period 6 shown here. Moving right within a row keeps the shell count fixed but adds one more electron to the outermost shell each step.

Tap down a column (a group) instead, and something different happens: the outermost-shell electron count stays the same, but a whole new shell is added underneath it. That is why elements in the same group — sodium (Z = 11) and potassium (Z = 19), for instance — share a family resemblance. Both have exactly one electron in their outer shell, so both lose that one electron easily and form similar compounds, even though potassium is a whole shell bigger.

## Formal

The table is ordered by atomic number Z, but arranged in rows and columns so that chemically similar elements line up. A period is a row: all its elements have the same number of occupied electron shells. A group is a column: for the main-group elements (the tall columns on the far left and right), every element in it has the same number of valence electrons, the ones in the outermost shell that take part in bonding.

Elements are grouped into families that share this valence structure: the alkali metals (group 1, one valence electron, very reactive), the alkaline earth metals (group 2), the halogens (group 17, one electron short of a full shell, also very reactive), and the noble gases (group 18, a full outer shell, almost inert). Reactivity and outer-shell electron count are two sides of the same coin.

## Advanced

Across a period, atomic size shrinks even though electrons are being added, because the nuclear charge Z grows at the same time and pulls the (roughly constant) inner shielding electrons in more tightly; down a group, size grows because each new shell sits further from the nucleus. These two trends — one across, one down — are the skeleton on which every other periodic trend (ionisation energy, electronegativity) is built, and the next lesson looks at them directly.

## Derivation

1. The identity of an element is fixed by its proton count; in a neutral atom the electron count matches it.
2. Electrons fill shells one at a time, and a full shell starts a new period.
3. The outermost, partially filled shell holds the valence electrons that give a group its shared chemistry.

## Real world

### Predicting reactions before you run them
Chemists predict how a new element or compound will behave just from its position in the table, long before testing it in a lab.

### Reading the table like a map
Metals cluster on the left and bottom-left, nonmetals on the top right, with a diagonal staircase of metalloids between them — a first clue to conductivity and bonding.

### Why sodium and potassium react alike
Both sit in group 1: one loosely held valence electron makes both soft, shiny metals that react violently with water.

### Discovering new elements
Mendeleev's table had gaps; the trends were so reliable that he predicted the properties of undiscovered elements before they were found.
