## Try it

1. Set N to 9, then to 10. Nine small squares are replaced by one rod. Each rod represents ten ones.
2. Compare 99 and 100. At 100, the tens and ones columns are empty and one hundred-square appears.
3. Compare 347 and 743. The tens column stays the same. What changes in the hundreds and ones columns?

## Real-life examples

1. **Packing 124 pencils.** You can make one group of 100 and two groups of 10, with 4 pencils left.
2. **Counting 2530 seats.** The number contains 2 thousands, 5 hundreds and 3 tens. The zero tells us there are no extra ones.
3. **Counting 8000 tickets.** Eight packs of 1000 tickets make 8000. The hundreds, tens and ones digits are all zero.

## Test yourself

1. Set N to 1000. Which column contains a block?
2. Choose a number with 5 in the hundreds place.
3. Choose a number greater than 9000 and less than 9500.

## Intuition

Suppose you have 347 pencils. You can group them into 3 hundreds, 4 tens and 7 ones. The digit 3 tells you how many hundreds there are, so it contributes 300 to the total. This is place value: a digit's position tells you what it is worth.

The blocks show these groups. A small square represents one, a rod represents ten, and a large square represents one hundred. A block in the thousands column represents one thousand. Ten of any one group have the same value as one group in the next column to the left.

Now compare 347 and 743. In 743, the 7 means 700 and the 3 means 3. The digits are the same, but their values have changed. The total increases by 396: the hundreds add 400, while the ones decrease by 4.

## Formal

Our decimal system uses ten digits, from 0 to 9. Starting on the right, the places represent ones, tens, hundreds and thousands. Each place is worth ten times the place immediately to its right.

For example, $347 = 3\times10^{2} + 4\times10^{1} + 7\times10^{0}$. Here the powers of ten mean 100, 10 and 1. Adding the three contributions gives the whole number.

## Advanced

Other bases use the same principle. In base $b$, the digits range from $0$ to $b-1$. Counting places from zero on the right, place $i$ has value $b^{i}$. Binary uses $b=2$, while decimal uses $b=10$.

## Derivation

1. Separate 347 into 300, 40 and 7. Adding these parts gives the original number.
2. Write 300 as 3 times 100, and 40 as 4 times 10. The 7 counts individual ones.
3. Use powers of ten to write the place values more compactly: $100 = 10^2$ and $10 = 10^1$.

## Real world

### Money

An amount of 3.47 has 3 whole currency units, 4 tenths and 7 hundredths. Place value also works to the right of the decimal point.

### Counting beyond nine

When a counter goes from 9 to 10, the ones digit returns to zero and the tens digit increases by one. You have counted one more item, not lost the previous nine.

### Computer storage

A binary digit, called a bit, is either 0 or 1. In a binary whole number, the places are worth 1, 2, 4, 8 and so on.
