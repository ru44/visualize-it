## Try it

1. Set e to 0. The length is 1 metre. The picture gives a nearby size comparison, not an exact measurement of that object.
2. Set e to 1, then 2. The lengths are 10 metres and 100 metres. Each increase of 1 multiplies the length by ten.
3. Set e to -1, then -2. The lengths are 0.1 metre and 0.01 metre. Negative exponents give fractions of a metre, not negative lengths.

## Real-life examples

1. **A nanometre.** At e = -9, the length is one billionth of a metre. The DNA icon is a nearby size comparison.
2. **One metre.** At e = 0, the length is 1 metre. This is a useful starting point for comparing everyday sizes.
3. **Ten million metres.** At e = 7, the length is 10,000,000 metres. The Earth icon helps suggest this scale; the number is not its exact diameter.

## Test yourself

1. Set e to 3, giving a length of 1000 metres.
2. Choose e between -3 and -1, including the endpoints.
3. Set the length to one million metres.

## Intuition

Start with a length of 1 metre. Multiply by ten to get 10 metres, and multiply by ten again to get 100 metres. Powers of ten let us record those repeated steps without writing a long string of multiplications.

In $10^{e}$, e is called the exponent. At e = 3, we multiply 1 by ten three times to get 1000. Going the other way reverses the operation: e = -1 gives 0.1, and e = -2 gives 0.01. Every result is positive.

The ruler measures the exponent, not the length directly. Equal steps along it represent equal ratios: each increase of 1 makes the length ten times as large. Use whole-number settings for the introductory examples. The nearby icons are scale comparisons, not pictures drawn at their actual relative sizes.

## Formal

For a positive whole-number exponent, $10^{e}$ is the product of e factors of ten. The zero power is $10^0=1$. For a positive whole number n, $10^{-n}=1/10^n$.

Increasing the exponent by 1 gives $10^{e+1}=10\times10^e$. This rule also holds for non-integer exponents, although those cannot be described by counting a whole number of factors.

## Advanced

Real exponents extend the pattern continuously: $10^x=\exp(x\ln 10)$. For example, an increase of 0.5 multiplies a length by the square root of ten, rather than by five.

## Derivation

1. Begin at 1 metre. An exponent of zero represents this starting value.
2. One multiplication by ten gives 10; two give 100.
3. Each additional multiplication raises the exponent by 1.

## Real world

### Metric units

One kilometre is 1000 metres. One millimetre is 0.001 metre. Powers of ten connect these units.

### Comparing sizes

A length of 1000 metres is one thousand times a length of 1 metre. Writing the powers makes that ratio easier to see.

### Maps

At a scale of 1:100,000, one centimetre on the map represents 100,000 centimetres, or one kilometre, on the ground.
