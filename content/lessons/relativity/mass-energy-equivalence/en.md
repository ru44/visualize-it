## Try it

1. Watch the blue line rise as you raise x, the mass converted, in grams. The orange dot shows the energy released, in millions of kilowatt-hours.
2. Set x to 1 gram. The dot lands at 25: one gram of mass, fully converted, releases 25 million kWh, more than a small city uses in a day.
3. Push x to 10 grams. The dot climbs to 250 million kWh, a huge amount of energy from a mass you could hold on a spoon.

## Real-life examples

1. **A one-gram paperclip's worth of mass.** Converted completely, it releases 25 million kWh of energy.
2. **A milligram of dust.** Even this tiny mass, x = 0.001 g, would release 25,000 kWh if converted completely — enough to power a home for years.
3. **A small coin's worth of mass.** At x = 10 g, converting it fully would release 250 million kWh.

## Intuition

Raise the slider x, the mass converted in grams, and watch the blue line climb steeply while the orange dot tracks the energy released, in millions of kilowatt-hours. Even at x = 1, one gram, barely the weight of a paperclip, the dot already sits at 25 — 25 million kilowatt-hours from a single gram.

The line climbs so fast because mass and energy are, at bottom, the same thing measured in different units, and the conversion factor between them is enormous: the speed of light squared. That is why nuclear reactions, which convert only a tiny fraction of their fuel's mass into energy, still release far more energy than burning the same mass of coal or oil.

## Formal

Einstein's mass–energy relation is $E = mc^2$, where $m$ is the mass converted and $c$ is the speed of light; because $c$ is so large, even a small mass corresponds to a huge amount of energy $E$.

Converting one gram of mass gives $E \approx (0.001\,\text{kg})\,c^2 \approx 9\times10^{13}\,\text{J}$, and since $9\times10^{13}\,\text{J} \approx 25\times10^{6}\,\text{kWh}$, that single gram is worth about 25 million kilowatt-hours, the slope of the line on the graph.

## Advanced

Almost no everyday process converts mass to energy directly; chemical reactions like burning release energy from rearranging electron bonds, changing mass by a fraction too small to measure, while nuclear fission and fusion tap the much larger energy stored in the nucleus itself, converting a measurable fraction of a percent of the mass involved.

## Derivation

1. Special relativity relates mass and energy directly, $E = mc^2$, with the speed of light $c$ as the conversion factor.
2. Plugging in one gram of mass gives $E \approx (0.001\,\text{kg})\,c^2 \approx 9\times10^{13}\,\text{J}$, an enormous number of joules from a tiny mass.
3. Converting to everyday units, $9\times10^{13}\,\text{J} \approx 25\times10^{6}\,\text{kWh}$, matching the 25-per-gram slope plotted here.

## Real world

### The Sun
Every second, the Sun converts about 4 million tonnes of its own mass into the energy that reaches Earth as sunlight, a conversion this graph's line would need to stretch far beyond its slider to show.

### Nuclear power plants
A nuclear reactor generates enormous amounts of electricity from a relatively small amount of fuel, because splitting uranium nuclei converts a measurable sliver of their mass directly into energy.

### Particle physics
When particles and their antiparticles meet, their entire mass converts to energy in one burst, the cleanest real-world example of $E = mc^2$ in action.

### Why chemical fuels can't compete
Burning a gram of gasoline releases only about 0.00005 million kWh, roughly half a million times less than converting that same gram's mass directly, because chemical reactions barely touch the mass at all.
