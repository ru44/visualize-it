## Try it

1. With «speed as a fraction of c» near 0, the two rods on screen — "at rest" and "passing by" — are almost the same length.
2. Raise «speed as a fraction of c» toward 1. The bottom rod visibly shrinks while the top one stays fixed, and gamma climbs in the readouts.
3. Watch the two light clocks on the right: the moving one's photon ticks slower, in step with the same gamma that is shrinking the rod below it.

## Real-life examples

1. **A 10 m rod barely moving.** At 10% of the speed of light the contraction is under 1% — far too small to notice in everyday life.
2. **The same rod at 60% of light speed.** Now the shrink is unmistakable: the rod passing by measures noticeably less than 10 m.
3. **A 15 m ship near the speed of light.** At 95% of c the ship is squeezed to a small fraction of its resting length, exactly what particle accelerators confirm every day.

## Test yourself

1. Set the speed so that gamma reads almost exactly 2.
2. Find a speed and proper length that squeeze the moving rod down to about 5 m.
3. Push the speed to 98% of the speed of light.

## Intuition

A light clock ticks once every time a photon makes the round trip between two mirrors. Watch the two little clocks on the right: at rest, the photon bounces at a steady pace. Send that same clock past you at high speed and — this picture speeds it up artificially so you can see it — from your side of the platform each of its ticks visibly takes longer, because the photon now has to cover a longer, diagonal path to catch up with the moving mirror.

A rod carried alongside that same clock has to shrink for the two of you to agree on when the photon arrives at the far mirror. Drag «speed as a fraction of c» up and watch the bottom rod, "passing by", pull in below the fixed top rod, by exactly the factor gamma that is slowing the moving clock in the first place.

## Formal

The Lorentz factor is $\gamma=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1-v^2/c^2}}$, where $v$ is the object's speed and $c$ the speed of light; $\gamma$ equals 1 at rest and grows without bound as $v$ approaches $c$.

A light clock of proper length $L_0$ ticks once every $t_0 = \dfrac{L_0}{c}$ in its own rest frame; seen from outside that tick takes $t=\gamma\,t_0$, and consistency between the two views forces the rod itself to measure $L = \dfrac{L_0}{\gamma}$ along its direction of motion.

## Advanced

Length contraction only affects the dimension parallel to the motion — a fast-moving sphere becomes a flattened ellipsoid, never a smaller sphere — and it is exactly reciprocal: an observer riding on the rod sees it at its full length $L_0$ and instead sees the platform, and everything on it, contracted by the same factor $\gamma$.

## Derivation

1. The Lorentz factor gamma grows from 1 at rest toward infinity as the speed approaches the speed of light.
2. A light clock at rest ticks once every L₀/c; the same clock, watched from outside while moving, ticks gamma times slower.
3. For the two frames to agree about the photon's arrival, the moving rod's length must shrink by the same factor gamma.

## Real world

### Particle accelerators
Protons in the Large Hadron Collider reach over 99.9999% of the speed of light, squeezed by a gamma in the thousands — exactly the effect this picture shows, scaled up enormously.

### Cosmic ray muons
Fast-moving muons created high in the atmosphere reach the ground far more often than their short lifetime alone would allow, partly because the atmosphere itself appears contracted from the muon's point of view.

### GPS satellites
Satellite clocks move fast enough that engineers must correct for relativistic effects daily, or GPS positions would drift by kilometres within a day.

### Nothing at everyday speed
A car on a highway contracts by roughly one part in $10^{14}$ — the effect is completely real, just far too small for any ruler to catch.
