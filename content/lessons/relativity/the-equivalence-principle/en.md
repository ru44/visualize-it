## Try it

1. Watch the ball, tossed sideways at speed u, fall inside the accelerating elevator. At a = 9.81 m/s², Earth's own gravity, it curves down and hits the floor before it ever reaches the far wall, 2.4 m away.
2. Drop a to 1.62 m/s², the Moon's gravity. The same toss now barely dips before reaching the far wall — weaker "gravity" curves the path far less.
3. Look at the tiny second curve traced for the light beam crossing the same cabin. It bends by the same physics, but by an amount labelled in the readout as a fraction of a proton's width — real, but far too small to draw to scale.

## Real-life examples

1. **An elevator accelerating at exactly 9.81 m/s².** A ball tossed at 2.5 m/s falls to the floor after crossing only about 1.75 m of the 2.4 m cabin — indistinguishable from tossing a ball in an ordinary room on Earth.
2. **An elevator accelerating at 1.62 m/s²**, the Moon's own surface gravity. The same 2.5 m/s toss barely sags by 0.75 m over the full crossing, the same gentle arc an astronaut's toss would trace while standing on the Moon.
3. **A strong 20 m/s² acceleration with a slow 1 m/s toss.** The ball drops to the floor after moving only about 0.5 m sideways — a dramatically steep, fast fall, like tossing a ball on a planet with gravity twice Earth's.

## Test yourself

1. Set a to Earth's own gravity, 9.81 m/s².
2. Set a to the Moon's gravity, 1.62 m/s².
3. Push the ball's toss speed u to its maximum, 5 m/s.

## Intuition

Imagine floating in a windowless elevator, far from any planet, while a rocket underneath accelerates it upward through empty space. Toss a ball sideways and, from inside the elevator, it curves down and lands on the floor — not because anything pulled it down, but because the floor itself kept accelerating up to meet it. There is no experiment you could run inside that sealed elevator to tell that story apart from simply standing still in a gravitational field of the same strength: this is Einstein's equivalence principle.

Since the ball's path only depends on how long it is in the air, the very same curve applies to a beam of light crossing the same cabin — light also "falls" while it crosses, following $y = \tfrac{1}{2}at^2$ just like the ball. The only difference is time: light crosses in a few nanoseconds instead of the ball's fraction of a second, so its fall is billions of times smaller, far too small to see, but exactly as real.

## Formal

Inside an elevator accelerating at rate $a$, any object launched sideways at speed $u$ falls a height $y = \tfrac{1}{2}at^2$ by the time $t$ has passed, measured in the elevator's own frame — the same formula, and the same curve, as an object thrown sideways in an ordinary gravitational field of strength $g = a$.

A beam of light crossing a cabin of width $W$ takes only $t_{\text{cross}} = W/c$ to get across, so it falls $y_{\text{light}} = \tfrac{1}{2}a(W/c)^2$ in that time — the identical formula, evaluated at light's enormous speed instead of a ball's ordinary one.

## Advanced

Because $c$ is so much larger than any everyday speed $u$, the light beam's crossing time is shorter by the same huge factor, and its fall $y_{\text{light}}$ is smaller by that factor squared — which is why the light beam's curve in this picture, computed from real numbers, comes out far smaller than an atomic nucleus for a stationary lab elevator, even though the underlying physics bending it is identical to what curves the ball's path.

## Derivation

1. Inside an accelerating elevator, an object launched sideways at speed $u$ falls a height $y = \tfrac12 a t^2$ after time $t$, purely because the floor keeps accelerating up to meet it.
2. Crossing a cabin of width $W$ at speed $u$ takes $t_{\text{cross}} = W/u$, so a ball's fall by the far wall is $\tfrac12 a (W/u)^2$; a beam of light instead takes only $t_{\text{cross}} = W/c$.
3. Plugging light's crossing time into the very same formula gives $y_{\text{light}} = \tfrac12 a (W/c)^2 \ll y_{\text{ball}}$ — light bends by exactly the same physics, just by an amount too small to ever see in a stationary elevator on Earth.

## Real world

### The 1919 solar eclipse expedition
Arthur Eddington photographed stars near the Sun's edge during a total eclipse and found their light bent by almost exactly the angle Einstein's equivalence-based theory predicted — the first direct confirmation that gravity really does bend light, not just matter.

### Gravitational lensing
Massive galaxy clusters bend the light of galaxies far behind them enough to distort and even multiply their images, a large-scale, dramatically visible version of the same bending this elevator shows on too small a scale to see.

### Why astronauts feel "weightless" in orbit
Astronauts orbiting Earth are in free fall the whole time, constantly accelerating toward the planet; the equivalence principle explains why that feels exactly like floating in gravity-free space rather than like falling, since a freely falling elevator and a gravity-free one are, locally, indistinguishable.

### Rocket launches
Astronauts pressed back into their seats during a powerful rocket launch feel a force indistinguishable from extra gravity, which is exactly why launch acceleration is often described in everyday language as "g-force" — the same g as ordinary gravity, by the equivalence principle.
