## Try it

1. Watch the blue line rise as you raise x, the black hole's mass in multiples of our Sun. The orange dot shows the radius, in kilometres, of the event horizon — the point of no return.
2. Set x to 1, one solar mass. The dot lands under 3 km: our Sun would need to shrink to about that size to become a black hole.
3. Push x to 20. The horizon grows past 55 km, roughly the size of a large city.

## Real-life examples

1. **The Sun as a black hole.** If our Sun (x = 1) were compressed this small, its event horizon would stretch about 3 km across.
2. **A typical stellar black hole.** Many black holes astronomers detect form from collapsed stars around x = 10, with a horizon roughly 30 km across.
3. **A large stellar-mass black hole.** At the top of the slider, x = 20, the horizon has grown to nearly 60 km.

## Intuition

Raise the slider x, the mass in multiples of our Sun, and watch the blue line climb and the orange dot slide up with it. That dot marks the radius of the event horizon: a sphere around the mass where gravity is so strong that not even light moving at full speed can climb back out.

More mass packed the same way always means a bigger horizon — doubling x roughly doubles the horizon's size. What makes a black hole extreme is usually not its mass but how tightly that mass is squeezed: the Sun already has plenty of mass, it just is not small enough yet to have an event horizon of its own.

## Formal

An object becomes a black hole once its escape velocity, $v_{esc} = \sqrt{\dfrac{2GM}{r}}$, would need to reach or exceed the speed of light $c$ to leave its surface.

Setting $v_{esc} = c$ and solving for the radius gives the Schwarzschild radius, $r_s = \dfrac{2GM}{c^2}$; for a mass $M$ measured in solar masses, this works out to about $r_s \approx 2.95\,M$ kilometres, matching the line on the graph.

## Advanced

Nothing physical actually sits at radius $r_s$ — it is a mathematical boundary, the event horizon, not a surface; general relativity predicts the mass itself collapses further, to a point-like singularity where the equations of the theory stop making sense.

## Derivation

1. Escape velocity from a mass $M$ at radius $r$ is $v_{esc} = \sqrt{2GM/r}$, the speed needed to leave the surface for good.
2. Setting that escape velocity equal to the speed of light and solving for the radius gives the Schwarzschild radius, $r_s = 2GM/c^2$.
3. For a mass measured in solar masses, this works out to a simple rule of thumb, $r_s \approx 2.95\,M$ kilometres, the line plotted here.

## Real world

### The Event Horizon Telescope, 2019
Astronomers combined radio telescopes across the whole planet to capture the first direct image of a black hole's silhouette, in the galaxy M87, confirming the size Einstein's equations predicted.

### Earth as a black hole
If Earth's entire mass were squeezed into a black hole, its event horizon would be barely 9 millimetres across, smaller than a grape.

### Stellar collapse
When a massive star runs out of fuel, its core can collapse so completely that nothing, not even the star's own light, can stop the crush past a certain point, forming a stellar-mass black hole.

### Sagittarius A*
At the centre of our own galaxy sits a black hole with a mass of about 4 million Suns, far beyond this slider's range, yet its horizon is still tiny compared with the vast orbits of stars swirling around it.
