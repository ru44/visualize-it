## Try it

1. Set n to 19, the number Aristarchus found, and leave cover at 100. In the bottom strip, find the tiny Earth and the Sun beside it. The Sun is already several Earths wide.
2. Raise n to 400. The Sun in the bottom strip swells until only the edge of it fits on the screen, and the label says how many Earths wide it is.
3. Move cover from 100 down to 0. The Moon slides off the Sun in the sky view, and nothing else changes: the sizes depend only on n.

## Real-life examples

- **Aristarchus, about 250 BC.** He had n = 19. The Moon is 3,474 km wide, so the Sun is 19 × 3,474, about 66,000 km. That is 5 Earths side by side, and it was far too small, but it was already bigger than the whole known world.
- **Today.** The Sun looks about 3 % bigger than the average Moon, so n = 400. That gives 1.39 million km, 109 Earths side by side. It is the real width of the Sun.
- **A partial eclipse.** Same sizes, but the Moon has covered only half of the Sun. The shape you see changes, the size of the Sun does not.

## Test yourself

1. Make the Sun 1.39 million km wide, the real size, within 2 %.
2. Make the Sun exactly about 109 Earths wide.
3. Make a Sun smaller than the Earth. It is easy to do, but nobody after Aristarchus could believe a Sun that small, because the numbers of the sky say it is not.

## Intuition

On 21 August 2017, millions of people across the United States stood in the Moon's shadow and watched it slide across the Sun until noon turned to dusk. In a total solar eclipse the Moon fits over the Sun like a coin over another coin. The two look the same size in the sky: each is about half a degree wide. Look at the sky view in the middle of the picture: with cover at 100, the Moon fits the Sun exactly.

Two things that look equally big but are not equally far must be very different in size. The Sun is farther than the Moon by a number n. Draw a cone from your eye through the edge of the Moon and keep going: at the Sun it is n times wider, so the Sun is n times wider than the Moon. The top of the picture shows this cone. Move n and watch the Sun disc grow to fill it.

The earlier lesson gave you n. Aristarchus of Samos measured it as 19 around 250 BC. The truth is about 400. The Moon is 3,474 km wide, so 19 gives 66,000 km, about 5 Earths side by side. And 400 gives 1.39 million km, 109 Earths side by side. The bottom strip draws the Earth, the Moon and your Sun at true relative sizes, and it clips the Sun when it does not fit.

Even Aristarchus's too-small number said something startling: the Sun is much bigger than the Earth. That made it hard to believe that the huge Sun goes round the small Earth. So he suggested that the Earth goes round the Sun. That was about 1,800 years before Copernicus, and almost nobody believed him.

## Formal

A total solar eclipse shows that the Sun and the Moon have almost the same angular size as seen from Earth. Let $D_S$ and $D_M$ be their widths and $d_S$ and $d_M$ their distances. The same angle gives similar triangles, so $\frac{D_S}{d_S} = \frac{D_M}{d_M}$. Writing $n = d_S / d_M$ for the ratio of distances, the Sun is $n$ times wider: $D_S = n\,D_M$.

With $D_M = 3{,}474$ km, $n = 19$ gives about 66,000 km, and the real value of $n$, about 400, gives 1.39 million km. The two ratios are not exactly equal. The distance ratio averages 389, while the width ratio is about 400, because the Sun looks about 3 % bigger than the average Moon. A total eclipse happens when the Moon is nearer than average and looks bigger.

## Advanced

The Moon's distance changes by about 12 % along its orbit, from about 363,000 to 405,000 km, so its angular width runs from about 0.49 to 0.56 degrees. The Sun's changes by only 3 %. When the Moon looks smaller than the Sun, it cannot cover it and we see a ring of light, an annular eclipse. The estimate $D_S = n\,D_M$ is therefore best made from the average sizes, not from one eclipse.

His n was too small because the half-moon angle he measured, 87°, is really 89.85°, too close to a right angle to measure by eye. His method was right, and his eyes were not good enough. Better measurements of the Sun's distance came much later, with transits of Venus and with radar.

## Derivation

1. The Sun and the Moon fill the same angle in the sky, so the triangle from your eye to the Sun's edges and the one from your eye to the Moon's edges are similar. Width over distance is the same for both.
2. The Sun is $n$ times farther away than the Moon, so $d_S = n\,d_M$.
3. Put this into the first line. The widths must be in the same ratio as the distances: $D_S = n\,D_M$.

## Real world

### Why total eclipses are rare

The Moon is only just big enough to cover the Sun. When it is a little too far away, it leaves a bright ring around the edge, and that is called an annular eclipse.

### The ratio that happens by luck

The Sun is about 400 times wider than the Moon and about 400 times farther away. This near match, which is a coincidence, is why eclipses look so perfect. In the far past the Moon was closer and looked bigger, and in the far future it will look too small.

### A million Earths

A ball 109 times wider than the Earth would hold about 1.3 million Earths. The Sun holds that, and it is a medium-sized star.

### Seeing the Sun safely

Never look at the Sun, even when the Moon covers most of it. Use eclipse glasses, or a pinhole in a card that throws a small picture of the Sun on the ground.
