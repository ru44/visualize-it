## Try it

1. Start at rest: 70 beats per minute, 70 mL per beat. Watch the loop take about a minute to complete one full circuit.
2. Raise heart rate toward a jogging pace and watch the loop speed up.
3. Push both heart rate and stroke volume to a trained athlete's maximum effort. Watch cardiac output blow past 20 L/min and the loop complete in barely 12 seconds.

## Real-life examples

1. **Resting on the couch.** 70 beats per minute, 70 mL per beat — about 4.9 L/min, close to your whole blood volume moving once every minute.
2. **A moderate jog.** Heart rate climbs to about 140 bpm, and stroke volume rises too as the heart fills and empties more forcefully — output more than doubles, to about 12.6 L/min.
3. **An elite athlete at maximum effort.** A trained heart near 190 bpm, with a stroke volume built up by years of training to about 130 mL, can push cardiac output past 24 L/min — five times the resting rate.

## Test yourself

1. Land close to a resting cardiac output of about 5 L/min.
2. Reach an elite athlete's cardiac output of about 25 L/min.
3. Get heart rate above 150 and stroke volume above 90 at the same time.

## Intuition

Picture the heart as a pump doing the same simple job over and over: fill up, squeeze, push a measured amount of blood out. How much it pushes out with one squeeze is the stroke volume; how many times a minute it squeezes is the heart rate. Multiply the two and you get exactly how much blood leaves the heart every minute — nothing hidden, no extra physics, just repeated squirts adding up.

That is why both numbers matter, not just one. A racing heart that barely fills between beats can move less blood than a slower, stronger one — which is part of why a trained athlete's heart, beating unusually slowly at rest, is not a weak heart but an efficient one: each beat already moves a big, well-practised stroke volume.

## Formal

Cardiac output is the product of the two, $\mathrm{CO} = \mathrm{HR}\times \mathrm{SV}$, usually reported in millilitres per minute and then divided by 1000 for litres per minute — a resting adult sits close to $\mathrm{CO}\approx 5$ L/min, almost exactly one full circuit of the roughly 5 litres of blood in the body.

Because $\mathrm{CO}$ is a product, either factor rising raises it: a faster heart rate alone can roughly double output, and a fitter heart that also raises its stroke volume under exertion can multiply the two effects together, which is how elite endurance athletes reach cardiac outputs several times the resting value.

## Advanced

Stroke volume itself is not fixed — the more blood fills the heart's chambers between beats, the more forcefully the heart muscle contracts and the more it ejects, a built-in mechanical response called the Frank–Starling mechanism. Endurance training enlarges the heart's chambers over months, raising the *resting* stroke volume permanently — which is exactly why a well-trained resting heart rate can sit as low as 40–50 bpm and still deliver a completely normal cardiac output.

## Derivation

1. One heartbeat ejects a stroke volume $\mathrm{SV}$ of blood from the heart into circulation.
2. The heart repeats that ejection $\mathrm{HR}$ times every minute.
3. Multiplying the two gives cardiac output $\mathrm{CO} = \mathrm{HR}\times \mathrm{SV}$ in mL/min, or $\mathrm{CO}/1000$ in L/min.

## Real world

### Resting heart rate as a fitness marker
Doctors and fitness trackers alike watch resting heart rate because a lower one, at the same cardiac output, usually means a stronger heart moving more blood with every single beat.

### Heart failure
In heart failure the weakened muscle cannot eject as much blood per beat, so stroke volume drops; the heart often compensates by beating faster, but cardiac output can still fall short of what the body needs.

### The fight-or-flight response
A surge of adrenaline raises heart rate almost instantly and makes each beat squeeze harder, boosting cardiac output within seconds to get more oxygen to the muscles.

### Wearable fitness trackers
Most fitness watches measure heart rate directly from your pulse and estimate cardiac output indirectly, using it to judge exercise intensity even though stroke volume itself is much harder to measure without a hospital.
