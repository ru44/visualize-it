## Try it

1. Watch the two lines cross. That crossing point is the market price and quantity everyone actually trades at.
2. Drag $a$ up. The demand line shifts up, and the crossing point moves to a higher price and a larger quantity.
3. Now drag $c$ up instead. The supply line shifts up, and the crossing point settles at a higher price but a smaller quantity.

## Real-life examples

1. **A booming city's housing market.** New tech jobs pull in residents willing to pay much more for a home (a high, flat b), while landlords are slow to add new supply (a higher c, flat d) — rents settle at a new, higher equilibrium.
2. **An oil supply shock.** A war disrupts a major oil producer: sellers now need a much higher price to supply any oil at all (c and d both rise sharply), so the same demand now clears only at a far higher price.
3. **A bumper harvest at a farmers' market.** A great growing season means farmers will sell even at a low price (c low, flat d), while buyers' appetite hasn't grown — the equilibrium price falls and more produce sells.

## Test yourself

1. Find values that put the equilibrium quantity at exactly 30 units.
2. Shift the demand curve so the equilibrium price rises above 60 while keeping the equilibrium quantity below 25.
3. Find values that put the equilibrium quantity at exactly 20 units while keeping the equilibrium price below 30.

## Intuition

Imagine a farmers' market for tomatoes: buyers would take 80 crates a week if they were free, but each extra dollar on the price puts off some buyers — that relationship is the demand line on screen. Farmers won't sell a single crate below 10 dollars, but bring more to market for every extra dollar offered — that's the supply line. Drag $a$, the highest price any buyer would pay, up: the whole demand line shifts up, and the point where the two lines cross — the price and quantity everyone actually trades at — moves to a higher price and a larger quantity.

Now drag $c$, the lowest price any seller accepts, up instead: the supply line shifts up, so farmers need a higher price before they'll sell at all, and the crossing point settles at a higher price but a smaller quantity. No one plans this outcome — at any other price, one side would want to trade more than the other side offers, and the price keeps moving until that gap closes.

## Formal

Demand is $P = a - b\,Q$: price falls in a straight line as quantity rises.
Supply is $P = c + d\,Q$: price rises in a straight line as quantity rises.
Equilibrium is the pair $(P^*, Q^*)$ where both hold at once.

Setting the right-hand sides equal, $a - bQ = c + dQ$, gives the
equilibrium quantity, and substituting it back into either line gives the
equilibrium price.

## Advanced

Any event that is not a price change — income, tastes, input costs,
technology, weather — shifts an entire line rather than moving a point
along it. Economists keep this distinction strict: a *movement along* the
demand curve is caused by price; a *shift of* the curve is caused by
everything else. How sharply quantity reacts to price is a separate
question, covered by elasticity.

## Derivation

1. Write the demand line: price falls as quantity rises.
2. Write the supply line: price rises as quantity rises.
3. Set them equal and solve for the quantity where both agree.

## Real world

### Housing rents
A city that adds many jobs shifts the demand curve right; rents rise until
a new, higher equilibrium is reached.

### Oil price shocks
A war cutting off a major producer shifts the supply curve left, so the
same quantity now clears only at a higher price.

### Farmers' markets
A bumper harvest shifts supply right: farmers accept a lower price to
sell the extra produce.

### Concert tickets
Fixed seating means supply barely moves with price, so a small rise in
demand can send prices up sharply.
