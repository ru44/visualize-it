## Try it

1. Look at the curve on screen: it shows how an electron's wavelength shrinks as its speed grows.
2. Drag the point along the curve toward higher speed. The wavelength reading drops fast.
3. Drag it back toward low speed and watch the wavelength grow instead, tracing the tangent line as you go.

## Real-life examples

1. **A slow electron beam.** At low speed the electron's wavelength is large enough to show up clearly on the plot.
2. **A faster electron beam.** Speeding the electron up shrinks its wavelength noticeably.
3. **An electron-microscope beam.** At the highest speeds shown here the wavelength shrinks toward the size of a single atom, which is exactly why electron microscopes can image things a light microscope never could.

## Test yourself

1. Set the speed so the wavelength reads about 7.3 on the plot.
2. Push the speed high enough that the wavelength drops below 1.
3. Land the wavelength on exactly 2.

## Intuition

A thrown ball has a wavelength too, by the same physics, but it is so many trillion times smaller than the ball itself that nothing about the ball ever looks wave-like. An electron is different: it is so light that its wavelength can be as large as an atom, so its wave side shows up everywhere, including in the double-slit stripes from the earlier lesson.

Drag the point along the curve on screen and read the wavelength number as the speed changes. A slow electron has a fairly long wavelength; the same electron sped up ten times has a wavelength ten times shorter, since speed and wavelength move in exact opposite directions.

## Formal

De Broglie proposed that any particle of momentum $p$ has a wavelength $\lambda = \dfrac{h}{p}$; since momentum for an everyday speed is $p = mv$, this becomes $\lambda = \dfrac{h}{mv}$.

Because Planck's constant $h$ is so small, only very light, fast-moving particles like electrons have a wavelength large enough to matter.

## Advanced

Louis de Broglie proposed matter waves in his 1924 doctoral thesis purely from symmetry with light, which by then was known to behave as both wave and particle; the idea was confirmed three years later when electrons scattering off a nickel crystal produced the same interference pattern X-rays give, proving electrons really do diffract like waves.

## Derivation

1. A faster or heavier particle, carrying more momentum, has a shorter wavelength.
2. For everyday speeds, momentum is simply mass times speed.
3. Combining the two, with Planck's constant as the constant of proportionality, gives the particle's wavelength.

## Real world

### Electron microscopes
Because fast electrons have a wavelength thousands of times shorter than visible light, electron microscopes can resolve details far finer than any light microscope.

### Electron diffraction in chemistry
Chemists fire electron beams at crystals and read the resulting diffraction pattern to work out where every atom sits.

### Neutron scattering
Neutron beams, which also carry a de Broglie wavelength, probe the atomic structure of materials too dense or opaque for light or electrons to penetrate.
